# WRAP Decision-Making

> A four-step process by Chip and Dan Heath for making better choices by overcoming the biases that distort everyday judgment.

**Book:** *Decisive: How to Make Better Choices in Life and Work* by Chip Heath and Dan Heath (2013)

---

## Core Concept

Most decisions are sabotaged by a predictable set of cognitive failures. The Heath brothers call them the **Four Villains of Decision Making**:

| Villain | What it does | WRAP counters with |
|---------|--------------|-------------------|
| **Narrow framing** | You see fewer options than actually exist (usually a binary "yes/no") | **W**iden your options |
| **Confirmation bias** | You collect data that supports what you already want to believe | **R**eality-test your assumptions |
| **Short-term emotion** | Your present feelings hijack a long-term choice | **A**ttain distance before deciding |
| **Overconfidence** | You assume you know how the future will play out | **P**repare to be wrong |

A pros-and-cons list does not solve any of these. WRAP is a process for noticing each villain and pushing back against it.

---

## The Four Steps

### 1. Widen Your Options

The first sign of a bad decision is the word "whether." Whenever you find yourself asking "should I do X or not," you've been ambushed by narrow framing.

**Vanishing options test.** Imagine the options you're currently considering disappeared. What would you do then? This forces you to generate alternatives you'd otherwise ignore.

**Find the opportunity cost.** Don't ask "is this worth it?" Ask "what else could I do with this time, money, or attention?" Compare against the next-best use, not against nothing.

**Multitrack.** Consider more than one option simultaneously. People who develop multiple options at once make better decisions and feel less attached to any single one. Aim for at least three real choices.

**Find someone who's solved your problem.** Look for an "inside view" (your past experience with similar decisions) and an "outside view" (people who have already made this kind of choice). Ask them what surprised them.

**AND not OR.** Look for ways to test multiple options instead of choosing between them. Can you do both, in smaller doses?

### 2. Reality-Test Your Assumptions

Once you have options, the next villain shows up: you start hunting for evidence to confirm what you already want.

**Consider the opposite.** Actively look for reasons your favored option is wrong. Ask: "What would have to be true for the other option to be the right choice?"

**Ask disconfirming questions.** Instead of "is this a good idea?" ask "how could this fail?" Replace leading questions with neutral ones.

**Zoom out: find the base rate.** What usually happens to people in this kind of situation? Statistics on similar decisions are more reliable than your gut about this one.

**Zoom in: get a close-up.** Find a concrete, textured example. Talk to someone living the reality of the choice you're considering. Visit the office, shadow the role, sit in the apartment.

**Ooch.** Run a small experiment before fully committing. Don't quit your job to start a business — moonlight first. Don't move cities — visit for a month. The goal is to get real data, not just imagine outcomes.

### 3. Attain Distance Before Deciding

Short-term emotion is a terrible decision-maker. The choice that feels urgent today often looks different in a week.

**10/10/10.** How will I feel about this decision in 10 minutes? In 10 months? In 10 years? Each timescale weights different considerations and shrinks any one feeling down to its real size.

**Advise a friend.** Ask: "What would I tell my best friend to do in this exact situation?" The advice you'd give them is usually clearer than the answer you'd give yourself, because you're not protecting your own ego.

**Identify your core priorities.** What are the long-term values you want this decision to serve? Write them down before you weigh options. When you know your priorities, conflicts get easier to resolve, because you know which side to favor.

**Honor your priorities by saying no.** Distance also means cutting things. Once you know what matters most, anything that doesn't serve it should be a candidate for elimination.

### 4. Prepare to Be Wrong

Even after the first three steps, you don't know the future. WRAP ends by accepting this and planning for it.

**Bookend the future.** Don't predict a single outcome. Instead, prepare for a range:
- **Pre-mortem.** Imagine it's a year from now and the decision failed. Write the story of why. What went wrong? This surfaces risks you'd otherwise miss.
- **Pre-parade.** Imagine it's a year from now and the decision succeeded beyond expectation. Are you ready for that? Success has its own logistics (capacity, scaling, attention) and people often fail to plan for it.

**Set tripwires.** Decide in advance what signal would tell you to reconsider, and when. Without tripwires, you'll drift. Examples: "If I'm not promoted by month 18, I'll start interviewing." "If we burn through 60% of runway without product-market fit, we change strategy."

**Trust the process.** A good process won't guarantee a good outcome (luck still matters), but it dramatically improves your odds and gives you something to stand on when results are mixed.

## Key Exercises and Tools

**Vanishing options test.** "Imagine the option I'm currently considering is off the table. What would I do?"

**Multitracking.** Generate at least three real options before evaluating any of them.

**Consider the opposite.** "What would have to be true for the other option to be right?"

**Zoom in / zoom out.** Look at base rates (statistics on similar choices) and a close-up (one real example, in detail).

**Ooching.** A small, time-boxed experiment that gives real-world data without full commitment.

**10/10/10.** "How will I feel about this in 10 minutes, 10 months, 10 years?"

**Advise a friend.** "What would I tell someone I love to do in this exact situation?"

**Pre-mortem.** "It's a year from now and this failed. What happened?"

**Pre-parade.** "It's a year from now and this worked spectacularly. Am I ready?"

**Tripwire.** A specific signal (date, metric, behavior) that triggers a reassessment.

## When to Use This Lens

WRAP is built for **consequential decisions** — the ones that are hard to reverse, expensive in time or money, or that shape years of your life. Examples:

- Career changes (taking a job, leaving a job, switching fields)
- Big purchases (house, car, large investment)
- Relationship questions (moving in, ending things, having a child)
- Life-direction choices (where to live, what to commit to, what to drop)
- Strategic bets (starting something, scaling something, killing something)

It is overkill for low-stakes daily choices. Use it when the decision will still matter a year from now.

## Integration with Daily Practice

### Surface the decision

Bring the decision into the **start-day** skill or a journal entry as soon as you notice it weighing on you. Naming it as a decision (not just a feeling) is the first move.

### Work it through with deep-dive

The **deep-dive** skill is the right home for WRAP. A decision worth this framework usually deserves an extended session, not a quick note. Walk through the four steps explicitly:

1. What options have I narrowly framed? What's my vanishing-options answer?
2. What assumptions am I making? What would disconfirm them? Can I ooch?
3. What does 10/10/10 say? What would I tell a friend? What core priorities does this serve?
4. What does the pre-mortem reveal? The pre-parade? What tripwires will I set?

A single deep-dive may not finish all four steps. That's expected. Decisions earn time.

### Track and revisit

Set tripwires explicitly in your journal so they survive past the moment. Reference them in **end-day** reviews when relevant ("did anything tripwire today?") and revisit them in weekly reviews.

### Don't decide tired

Short-term emotion is the third villain for a reason. If a decision is calling for resolution at 11pm, attain distance literally — sleep on it. Use the 10/10/10 question before committing.

## Key Questions

**Widen your options.**
- If my current options vanished, what would I do?
- What else could I do with the same time, money, or attention?

**Reality-test your assumptions.**
- What would have to be true for me to be wrong about this?
- What's the smallest experiment that could give me real data?

**Attain distance.**
- How will I feel about this in 10 months? 10 years?
- What would I tell a friend in this exact situation?

**Prepare to be wrong.**
- If this fails a year from now, what's the most likely reason?
- What signal will tell me to reconsider, and when?
