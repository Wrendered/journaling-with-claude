# Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT)

> A skills-based framework for handling intense emotions, distress, and difficult relationships. Built on the dialectic of accepting yourself as you are AND working to change.

**Origin:** Developed by Marsha Linehan in the late 1980s, originally for borderline personality disorder. Now widely applied to anxiety, depression, eating disorders, addiction, and general emotional regulation. The "dialectic" comes from Hegel: holding two opposing truths at once.

**Important caveat:** DBT is normally taught in a structured skills group alongside individual therapy. This file is a lens for self-reflection, not a substitute for clinical care. If you're in crisis or working with severe symptoms, find a DBT-trained therapist.

---

## Core Concept

DBT is built on a single tension: **you are doing the best you can, AND you need to do better.** Both are true at the same time. Most emotional suffering comes from refusing one side of that. You either beat yourself up for not being further along, or you give up on changing. The dialectic asks you to hold both — radical self-acceptance and a real commitment to growth.

This shows up most clearly in the **Wise Mind** model. You have an *emotion mind* (driven by feeling, hot, reactive) and a *reasonable mind* (driven by logic, cold, analytical). Wise Mind is the integration: the place where feeling and reason meet. Most bad decisions come from operating purely in one or the other. Wise Mind is the quiet, grounded knowing that emerges when both are listened to.

The skills below are tools for getting there. They aren't about suppressing emotion or "thinking your way out" of feelings. They're about widening the gap between stimulus and response so you can choose your next move instead of being run by it.

## The Four Modules

DBT organizes its skills into four areas. Mindfulness is the foundation — it underlies everything else.

### 1. Mindfulness (the foundation)

The capacity to notice what's happening, in you and around you, without immediately reacting.

**Wise Mind** — the integration of emotion mind and reasonable mind. Ask: "What does my Wise Mind say?" The answer often comes as a quieter voice underneath the loud ones.

**The "What" skills (what you do):**
- **Observe** — notice without words. Watch the breath, the sensation, the thought passing through.
- **Describe** — put words to what you observe. "I notice tightness in my chest." Stick to facts, not interpretations.
- **Participate** — fully enter the moment. Stop watching from outside; be in it.

**The "How" skills (how you do it):**
- **Non-judgmentally** — drop "good/bad/should/shouldn't." Things just are.
- **One-mindfully** — do one thing at a time. Eating is eating. Walking is walking.
- **Effectively** — focus on what works, not on being right.

### 2. Distress Tolerance

For when emotions are too intense to think through. The goal isn't to fix the situation — it's to get through it without making things worse.

**TIPP** (for emotional crisis, when you need to drop arousal fast):
- **T**emperature — cold water on the face, ice on the wrists. Triggers the dive reflex.
- **I**ntense exercise — 10-15 minutes hard. Burns off adrenaline.
- **P**aced breathing — exhale longer than you inhale (4 in, 6 out).
- **P**aired (progressive) muscle relaxation — tense and release each muscle group.

**ACCEPTS** (for distracting from distress without numbing):
- **A**ctivities, **C**ontributing (to others), **C**omparisons, **E**motions (opposite), **P**ushing away (mentally shelving), **T**houghts, **S**ensations.

**IMPROVE the moment** (when you can't change the situation):
- **I**magery, **M**eaning, **P**rayer, **R**elaxation, **O**ne thing at a time, **V**acation (a brief mental break), **E**ncouragement.

**Radical Acceptance** — the deepest distress tolerance skill. Reality is what it is. Fighting it adds suffering on top of pain. Acceptance is not approval. It's the choice to stop arguing with what's already true so you can decide what to do next.

### 3. Emotion Regulation

For changing emotions you don't want, and being less vulnerable to them.

**PLEASE** (reduce emotional vulnerability through the body):
- Treat **P**hysica**L** illness
- Balanced **E**ating
- Avoid mood-**A**ltering substances
- Balanced **S**leep
- **E**xercise

If you ignore these, your emotions will run hotter. No skill compensates for not sleeping.

**Check the Facts** — emotions often respond to your interpretation, not the event. Ask: what actually happened? What am I assuming? Does the emotion fit the facts as they are?

**Opposite Action** — when an emotion doesn't fit the facts, or when acting on it makes things worse, do the opposite of the urge. Fully, not half-heartedly.
- Fear says avoid → approach
- Anger says attack → gently withdraw
- Shame says hide → share with someone safe
- Sadness says isolate → engage

**Build Mastery** — do something hard each day that makes you feel capable. Small wins compound.

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness

Three goals in any interaction: get what you need, keep the relationship, keep your self-respect. Different acronyms for different goals.

**DEAR MAN** (for getting what you need):
- **D**escribe the situation factually
- **E**xpress your feelings using "I" statements
- **A**ssert clearly what you want
- **R**einforce (explain the benefit)
- **M**indful (stay on track, don't get pulled off)
- **A**ppear confident
- **N**egotiate

**GIVE** (for preserving the relationship):
- **G**entle, **I**nterested, **V**alidate, **E**asy manner.

**FAST** (for self-respect):
- **F**air (to yourself and them), **A**pologies (no unnecessary ones), **S**tick to your values, **T**ruthful.

## Key Exercises for Self-Reflection

**Wise Mind reflection** — when stuck on a decision, write out what emotion mind is saying, then what reasonable mind is saying. Then sit and ask: "What does my Wise Mind know?" Wait for it. The answer is usually quieter and more settled than either of the first two.

**Radical acceptance journal prompt** — "What am I refusing to accept right now? What would change if I stopped fighting it?" Acceptance doesn't mean liking it. It means stopping the second arrow.

**Opposite Action check** — name the emotion. Name the urge it's giving you. Ask: "Does acting on this urge fit the facts? Will it make things better or worse?" If the answer is worse, name the opposite action and do it.

**PLEASE check** — at the end of a hard day, ask: how did I sleep, eat, move today? Was I sick or running on fumes? Often what looks like an emotional problem is a body problem.

**Check the facts prompt** — "What story am I telling about this? What are the actual facts? What's interpretation?" Useful when an emotion feels disproportionate.

## When to Use This Lens

- When emotions feel too big to think through. (Distress Tolerance, TIPP.)
- When you're in conflict and don't know how to ask for what you need. (DEAR MAN, GIVE, FAST.)
- When you keep reacting in ways you regret. (Opposite Action, Wise Mind.)
- When something has happened that you can't change and you keep relitigating it. (Radical Acceptance.)
- When willpower-based approaches keep failing. DBT assumes you're not failing because you're weak; you're failing because you don't have the skill yet.
- When you notice the same emotional pattern repeating across situations.

DBT is especially useful when other frameworks feel too "rational." Stoicism, atomic habits, and CBT all work better when your nervous system is regulated. DBT is the layer underneath that gets you regulated.

## Integration with Daily Practice

**Morning (start-day skill):** A quick PLEASE check works as a vulnerability scan. Did I sleep enough? Am I sick, hungry, or running on caffeine? If yes, expect emotions to run hotter today and lower the bar accordingly. Naming a Wise Mind intention for the day can also anchor you: "What would the wisest version of me focus on today?"

**Evening (end-day skill):** If the day had a hard moment, journal it through the DBT lens. What emotion came up? What did I do with it? Was that opposite action or emotion-driven action? Did the emotion fit the facts? This is also a natural place to practice radical acceptance for things the day brought that you can't change.

**Deep dives (deep-dive skill):** Use DBT when a recurring emotional pattern keeps showing up. Map it: what triggers it, what story you tell, what urge it generates, what you usually do, what opposite action would look like. The pattern often loosens just from being seen clearly.

**Weekly review:** Look across the week. Which emotions ran the show? Where did Wise Mind show up, and where did emotion mind take over unchallenged? Were there moments where radical acceptance would have shortened the suffering?

## Key Questions

**For intense emotion:**
- What is this emotion telling me?
- Does it fit the facts, or does it fit a story?
- What's the urge? What would opposite action look like?

**For stuck situations:**
- What am I refusing to accept?
- What changes if I stop fighting reality and start from where things actually are?

**For decisions:**
- What does emotion mind say? What does reasonable mind say?
- What does Wise Mind know underneath both?

**For relationships:**
- What do I actually need here? (DEAR MAN)
- How do I want to treat them? (GIVE)
- How do I stay honest with myself? (FAST)

**For vulnerability:**
- How's my PLEASE today? Sleep, food, body, substances, movement?
- Am I trying to skill my way out of something a nap would fix?
