# Internal Family Systems (IFS)

**Purpose:** Offer an optional vocabulary for conflicting wishes, emotions, and
self-criticism. In this repository, parts language is a conversational lens for
present experience. Using it does not establish that a literal inner system has
been discovered or that clinical IFS therapy is taking place.

## Model and assumptions

Richard Schwartz's IFS model describes a person as having “parts” and a core
“Self.” It proposes positive underlying intentions for parts and a capacity for
Self leadership associated with curiosity and compassion. The model groups some
parts as managers (anticipatory protection), firefighters (reactive protection),
and exiles (vulnerability). These are the model's categories, not independently
established universal facts about minds. The IFS Institute explicitly presents
its premises as assumptions in its
[model outline](https://ifs-institute.com/resources/articles/internal-family-systems-model-outline).

Claims of an undamaged Self, a protective purpose behind every behavior, or a
particular childhood origin should remain attributed to the model. Neither a
compelling metaphor nor improvement during therapy demonstrates those claims.
The user may find ordinary words such as “a concern,” “an urge,” or “two wishes”
more accurate than parts terminology.

## When this lens fits

- The user already describes competing wishes as “part of me wants…”
- Naming more than one perspective could soften an all-or-nothing decision.
- The user wants to approach harsh self-talk with curiosity rather than argument.
- A tentative metaphor helps the user describe an experience in their own words.

These are proposed conversational uses, not evidence-based treatment indications.
Use a different lens when this vocabulary feels artificial, confusing, distancing,
or unnecessary. Logistics and external constraints often need direct action.

## What AI can borrow conversationally

Keep the work present-focused and user-led. Do not assign parts, ages, motives,
or histories. Ask one question and let the answer guide whether to continue.

1. **Reflect both sides:** Describe the competing wishes using the user's actual
   words. Ask whether the description fits before building on it.
2. **Offer optional distance:** If useful, try “I notice a strong urge to…” or
   “One part of me wants…” The user can keep “I feel…” if that feels truer.
3. **Explore the stated concern:** Ask what each side wants considered in the
   current decision. Do not presume that either side conceals a protector or wound.
4. **Find a response:** Look for an action, boundary, resource, or compromise that
   addresses the concerns the user actually identifies.
5. **Check usefulness:** Ask whether the language clarified the situation. Drop
   it when it adds complexity without helping.

For self-criticism, separate the criticism's content, tone, and consequences.
Consider whether it contains actionable information, then explore a less harmful
way to respond. A protective function is one possible hypothesis only if it fits
the user's account. Compassion does not require gratitude toward a hurtful voice.

## Pitfalls and limits

- Do not state that every coping behavior protects an exile, that strong reactions
  prove trauma, or that reluctance proves a resistant part.
- Do not ask the user to discover a part's age, search for forgotten events,
  visualize traumatic scenes, retrieve memories, or conduct “unburdening.” This
  reference does not guide trauma-processing procedures.
- Images, sensations, and imagined dialogue do not verify historical events.
  Preserve the difference between remembering, imagining, and hypothesizing.
- Do not treat discomfort or disagreement as evidence that another part is
  “blended.” The model must remain open to rejection.
- Parts language is neither a dissociative-disorder diagnosis nor evidence against
  one. If the exercise increases confusion, overwhelm, or detachment, stop the
  exercise and return to current surroundings and the user's preferred language.
- Keep responsibility attached to actions. A proposed protective intention does
  not excuse harm or establish someone else's hidden motives.

## Evidence scope

The IFS Institute describes its research base as limited and calls for replicated,
condition-specific randomized trials
([research page](https://ifs-institute.com/resources/research)). Its theoretical
and promotional accounts are primary sources for what IFS claims, not independent
confirmation of effectiveness.

In the 2026 PARTS randomized trial, 60 participants with PTSD received either an
IFS-based online program or an active comparison. Both conditions included
16 group sessions and eight individual sessions. Attendance and group satisfaction
favored PARTS; PTSD symptoms improved in both groups without a significant
between-group difference ([Joss et al., 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41609644/)).
This supports feasibility and acceptability in that setting, not superiority or
equivalence. It does not establish literal parts, the origin of a person's
feelings, or the effectiveness of AI-guided parts reflection.
