# Self-Compassion

A lens for responding to shame, harsh self-judgment, and performance pressure
with care while keeping room for responsibility and change.

## Purpose and source model

Kristin Neff's model brings together self-kindness, common humanity, and mindful
awareness of difficulty. It does not require a positive self-evaluation or
denial of pain. See [Neff's account of self-compassion](https://self-compassion.org/what-is-self-compassion/).

This page draws on that model and Mindful Self-Compassion (MSC) research.
Compassion Focused Therapy (CFT) is a distinct clinical approach developed by
Paul Gilbert, with a broader formulation and multiple therapeutic methods.
Borrowing compassionate language is not delivering CFT. See [Gilbert, Introducing Compassion-Focused Therapy (2009)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/S1355514600005654).

## Suitable uses

- A mistake has become a global verdict about the person's worth.
- The user wants to work toward something but harsh self-talk adds pressure.
- They can offer understanding to others but find it difficult for themselves.
- They want acknowledgment of pain before considering repair or a next step.

## Conversational adaptation for AI

These are local conversation choices, not an MSC course or a clinical protocol.

1. Name the actual difficulty in the user's words. Do not open with reassurance
   that everything will be fine or assume you know how they feel.
2. Offer a fairer stance toward the person while staying accurate about events.
   A mistake can matter without establishing that the person is worthless.
3. If useful, invite one compassionate perspective: “What would a fair, caring
   response to this difficulty sound like?” Let the user choose believable words.
4. Treat shared humanity as optional context. Do not imply that suffering is
   equivalent across people or that common experiences should hurt less.
5. Make support concrete if wanted: rest, asking for help, a boundary, repair,
   or a smaller next step. Compassion need not take the form of affirmations.
6. Check fit. If the language feels sentimental or false, try straightforward
   acknowledgment or a neutral description rather than more enthusiastic warmth.

An optional experiment is one sentence that states the difficulty without a
personal insult, followed by one realistic way to respond with care. It is
complete even if the user does not feel better immediately.

## Mismatches and pitfalls

- Do not demand self-love, gratitude, forgiveness, or a positive lesson.
  These can become further standards the user feels they are failing.
- Avoid using kindness to erase harm, excuse conduct, or bypass an apology.
  Care for oneself can coexist with accountability and care for others.
- Do not frame compassion only as a productivity technique. The person does
  not have to demonstrate improved performance to deserve humane treatment.
- Do not infer that discomfort with compassionate words reveals trauma or
  an unconscious block. Ask about the fit only if the user wants to explore it.
- Soothing touch, imagery, meditation, and closing the eyes are optional
  practices, never prerequisites for this conversational lens.

## Stop or switch

If the exercise increases shame, distress, or pressure, stop it. Do not insist
the person stay with it because discomfort supposedly means healing.
Offer plain acknowledgment, practical support, or a different lens they prefer.
When a fair response has landed, let it stand without requiring repeated phrases
or converting every moment of care into a behavior-change assignment.

## Evidence status

[Neff and Germer (2013)](https://self-compassion.org/wp-content/uploads/publications/Neff-Germer-MSC-RCT-2012.pdf)
reported a pilot and a randomized trial of an eight-week MSC course. The trial
compared 25 intervention participants with 27 waitlist participants and found
larger gains in self-compassion, mindfulness, and well-being. The sample was
small and mostly educated, middle-aged women; there was no active comparison.
This was community training evidence, not proof of treatment for every clinical
condition or of the effect of a single compassionate chat response.

[Breines and Chen (2012)](https://self-compassion.org/wp-content/uploads/publications/selfimp.motivation.pdf)
found increased self-improvement motivation across four experiments, including
more study time after a difficult test. These short experimental outcomes
support the possibility that compassion and improvement coexist. They do not
establish lasting behavior change or clinical efficacy.

Gilbert's paper describes CFT's clinical model; it is not a randomized trial.
This AI adaptation is unvalidated. Keep model descriptions, measured research
outcomes, and the user's actual experience of a prompt distinct.
