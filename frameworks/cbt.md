# Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT)

> A practical framework for noticing how thoughts shape feelings and actions, and gently testing the thoughts that cause unnecessary suffering.

**Origin:** Developed by Aaron T. Beck (psychiatrist, University of Pennsylvania) in the 1960s, building on related work by Albert Ellis (Rational Emotive Behavior Therapy). David Burns popularized the cognitive distortions list in *Feeling Good* (1980). Dennis Greenberger and Christine Padesky created the widely-used self-help workbook *Mind Over Mood*.

---

## Core Concept

**Thoughts, feelings, and behaviors are connected.** A situation doesn't directly cause a feeling. A thought about the situation does. Change the thought (or the behavior) and the feeling can shift.

The classic illustration is the cognitive triangle. Same situation, different thought, different result:

- Situation: A friend doesn't text back for a day.
- Thought A: "She's mad at me." → Feeling: anxious. → Behavior: avoid reaching out, ruminate.
- Thought B: "She's probably busy." → Feeling: neutral. → Behavior: get on with the day.

Beck noticed that depressed patients had a stream of "automatic thoughts" running underneath their feelings, often distorted and almost never examined. The work of CBT is to slow down, catch those thoughts, check them against reality, and find a more accurate, balanced view. Not "positive thinking" — accurate thinking. Sometimes the truth is hard. But often the automatic thought is more catastrophic, more personal, or more absolute than the situation warrants.

---

## Cognitive Distortions

These are the common shortcuts the mind takes that distort reality. Naming the pattern is half the work — once you see it, the thought loses some of its grip.

**1. All-or-Nothing Thinking (Black-and-White)**
Seeing things in extremes. No middle ground.
*"If I'm not the best, I'm a failure." "I had one bad meeting. The whole project is ruined."*

**2. Overgeneralization**
Treating one event as a never-ending pattern.
*"I bombed that interview. I'll never get a job."*

**3. Mental Filter**
Picking out a single negative detail and dwelling on it, ignoring everything else.
*Got nine compliments and one criticism on a presentation. Spends the night replaying the criticism.*

**4. Discounting the Positive**
Rejecting positive experiences as if they don't count.
*"They only said it was good because they're being nice."*

**5. Jumping to Conclusions**
Two flavors:
- **Mind-Reading:** Assuming you know what someone is thinking. *"He thinks I'm boring."*
- **Fortune-Telling:** Predicting things will turn out badly. *"This is going to fail."*

**6. Magnification or Minimization**
Blowing things out of proportion, or shrinking your strengths down to nothing. The extreme version of magnification is **catastrophizing**: imagining the worst-case outcome as inevitable.
*"This mistake will end my career."*

**7. Emotional Reasoning**
Treating feelings as facts.
*"I feel like a fraud, so I must be one." "I feel hopeless, so the situation is hopeless."*

**8. Should Statements**
Using "should," "must," "ought to" to flog yourself or others. Generates guilt when aimed inward, frustration when aimed outward.
*"I should be over this by now." "He shouldn't be like that."*

**9. Labeling**
Attaching a global negative label to yourself or someone else, instead of describing the behavior.
*"I'm a loser" instead of "I made a mistake." "He's a jerk" instead of "He was rude in that moment."*

**10. Personalization and Blame**
Holding yourself responsible for things outside your control, or blaming others while ignoring your own role.
*"My kid is struggling at school. I must be a bad parent."*

(Some versions of this list include an 11th — **Always Being Right**, treating being right as more important than being kind or honest. Burns' original ten are the ones you'll see most often.)

## Key Exercises

### Thought Record

The core CBT tool. Slow down a moment of distress and write through it. Greenberger and Padesky's seven-column version:

1. **Situation** — What happened? Who, what, where, when.
2. **Moods** — What did you feel? Rate intensity 0-100%.
3. **Automatic Thoughts** — What was running through your mind? Circle the "hot thought" — the one with the most charge.
4. **Evidence that supports the hot thought** — What facts back it up?
5. **Evidence against the hot thought** — What facts argue against it? What would you tell a friend?
6. **Alternative or balanced thought** — A new thought that takes all the evidence into account. Not forced positivity. Just truer.
7. **Re-rate moods** — How do you feel now?

You don't need a full worksheet every time. Even just naming the situation, the feeling, the thought, and one piece of counter-evidence can break the spiral.

### Behavioral Activation

When you're depressed or stuck, motivation usually shows up *after* action, not before. Behavioral activation flips the order: schedule small activities that historically gave you pleasure or a sense of mastery, and do them whether you feel like it or not. Track what shifts.

Often used as the first move with depression because rumination loosens its grip when the body moves.

### Socratic Questioning

Instead of arguing with a thought, ask questions that help you examine it. (See Key Questions below.)

### Behavioral Experiments

Take a belief and design a small test. *"If I speak up in the meeting, people will think I'm stupid."* Run the experiment. What actually happened? Beliefs hold less power once they meet evidence.

### Exposure

For anxiety and avoidance. Gradually face the thing you're avoiding, in small doses, long enough for the anxiety to peak and fall on its own. Avoidance keeps fear alive. Exposure teaches the nervous system that the threat is smaller than it felt.

### Activity and Mood Tracking

Note what you did and how you felt across the day. Patterns surface: which activities lift you, which drain you, which times of day are hardest. Use the data to design your week.

## When to Use This Lens

CBT is especially useful for:

- **Anxiety** — catastrophizing, fortune-telling, what-ifs
- **Depression** — the negative cognitive triad: bleak views of self, world, and future
- **Rumination** — getting stuck replaying the same thought
- **Harsh self-talk** — labeling, should statements, personalization
- **Avoidance** — when fear of a thought or feeling is shaping your behavior
- **Conflict** — mind-reading what the other person meant

Less useful when the issue is grief, trauma processing, or identity work that needs more than thought-checking. CBT pairs well with other approaches but doesn't replace them.

## Integration with Daily Practice

### Morning (start-day)

A light CBT check-in:
- Is there a thought I'm carrying into today that's worth examining?
- What's the worry underneath my MIT? Is it accurate?
- One reframe: "I have to" → "I get to," when it fits honestly.

### Evening (end-day)

Where the work tends to happen. When reviewing the day:
- What feeling stood out? What thought was running underneath it?
- Was there a distortion in there? (Name it.)
- What's a more balanced view, with the evidence I now have?

If something keeps coming up across days, write a quick thought record on it.

### Deep Dive

For thoughts that won't quit. A full thought record with all seven columns. Look for the deeper belief underneath the surface thought — often something like "I'm not enough" or "I'll be rejected." Name the pattern. Test it against the evidence of your actual life.

## Key Questions

The Socratic questions worth keeping close:

**Examining the thought:**
- What's the evidence for this thought? What's the evidence against it?
- Am I confusing a thought with a fact?
- Is there another way to look at this?
- What would I tell a friend who had this thought?

**Stress-testing it:**
- What's the worst that could happen? How would I cope if it did?
- What's the best that could happen? What's most likely?
- Will this matter in a week? A month? A year?
- Am I taking something personally that isn't about me?

**Naming the distortion:**
- Which distortion is this? (All-or-nothing? Mind-reading? Should?)
- Is this thought helpful, or just loud?
- What's the more accurate, balanced version?

**Moving forward:**
- If this thought were 50% less true, what would I do differently?
- What's one small action I can take from a more balanced place?

- *Feeling Great* — David D. Burns (2020). Updated approach with more recent tools.
