# Pre-Mortem

> Imagine your decision has already failed. Now figure out why.

**Source:** Gary Klein (originated). Detailed in his HBR article ["Performing a Project Premortem"](https://hbr.org/2007/09/performing-a-project-premortem) (2007), building on prospective hindsight research from the late 1980s. Popularized by Daniel Kahneman in *Thinking, Fast and Slow* (2011). Also referenced in *Decisive* by Chip and Dan Heath.

---

## Core Concept

A pre-mortem inverts the usual planning frame. Instead of asking "how do we make this work?", you assume the decision has already been made, time has passed, and the outcome was a disaster. Then you write the obituary.

The trick is **prospective hindsight**: imagining a future failure as if it has already occurred. This bypasses the optimism that floods most decisions. When something is hypothetical, the brain hedges. When it has "already happened," the brain produces concrete reasons.

It defeats overconfidence by giving permission to voice doubts. In group settings, the loudest concern in a normal planning meeting is "this might fail" — easily dismissed. In a pre-mortem, the framing is "it failed, so what happened?" — which makes specific, actionable failure modes safe to surface.

---

## How to Do It

A pre-mortem is fast. 15 to 30 minutes is plenty for a personal decision.

### 1. Make the decision concrete
Write down the decision as if you've committed to it. "I took the job at X." "I moved to Y." "I ended the relationship." Specific, past tense.

### 2. Fast-forward
Set a time horizon. One year is the default; pick whatever makes the outcome legible. Imagine that year has passed.

### 3. Imagine a disaster
The outcome is bad. Not mediocre, not mixed: a clear failure. Sit with that for a moment before writing.

### 4. Write the obituary
What went wrong? Get specific. Aim for 5 to 10 distinct failure modes. Push past the obvious ones. Examples of categories to probe:
- External shocks (market, health, family)
- Predictable patterns of yours (procrastination, isolation, conflict avoidance)
- Hidden assumptions that turned out wrong
- Things you knew were risks but rationalized away
- Second-order effects (what the decision made impossible)

### 5. Identify the most likely failures
Of the failure modes you listed, which feel most plausible? Which are you most prone to? Star the top 2 or 3.

### 6. Decide what to change now
For each starred failure mode, ask: what would I do differently to prevent this? This is the output. Concrete adjustments, not generic vigilance.

## Why It Works

Klein's research suggests prospective hindsight increases the ability to identify causes of future outcomes by roughly 30% compared to standard forecasting. The mechanism is simple: a definite outcome ("it failed") forces specific causal reasoning, while a probabilistic one ("it might fail") invites vague hedging.

It also reduces:
- **Overconfidence** — by treating failure as the premise, not a possibility
- **Groupthink** (in team settings) — by reframing dissent as analysis
- **Planning fallacy** — by surfacing the timeline and resource risks people normally suppress

---

## Pair With: Pre-Parade

Run the inverse before or after a pre-mortem. Imagine wild success. What had to go right? What did you do that you weren't expecting to do? Pre-parades surface what you actually want and what enabling conditions matter.

A pre-mortem alone can leave you risk-averse. A pre-parade alone can leave you blind. Together they give a balanced view: what failure looks like, what success requires, what's in the gap.

---

## When to Use

- Before any major decision — career, relationship, financial, geographic
- When you feel certain (certainty is the strongest signal you need this)
- When momentum or social pressure is pushing you toward yes
- When you've been deliberating for weeks but can't articulate the risks
- Before committing to a project, a launch, or a new system

Skip it for low-stakes decisions. The point is to slow down on choices that are hard to reverse.

---

## Integration with Daily Practice

Pre-mortem fits inside the **deep-dive** skill when working through a specific decision. Run it as a structured step:

1. State the decision in past tense
2. Walk through the obituary
3. Star the most likely failure modes
4. Output 1 to 3 concrete adjustments to make now

It can also pair with the **weekly-review** skill when an open decision has been sitting too long without a verdict — the pre-mortem often surfaces the actual blocker.
