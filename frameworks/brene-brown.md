# Brené Brown: shame, vulnerability, and values

**Type:** Research-informed educational lens. **Sources checked:** 2026-09-14.

Brown distinguishes judgments about the whole self from judgments about a behavior,
and explores vulnerability, belonging, and empathy. Her account of the research
describes qualitative grounded-theory work. These ideas offer vocabulary; they
do not constitute a diagnosis or establish a clinical treatment for this chat.
[Research account](https://brenebrown.com/the-research/),
[shame and guilt](https://brenebrown.com/articles/2013/01/15/shame-v-guilt/).

## Use in conversation

When the user names shame, explore what the feeling means to them before sorting
it into a category. They may be experiencing guilt, embarrassment, grief, longing,
or several things together. A possible prompt: “Is the painful thought about what
happened, what it says about you, or something else?”

If helpful, distinguish a specific responsibility from a global verdict about
worthiness. Notice cultural, interpersonal, and material context. An apology or
repair can matter when harm occurred; reassurance should not erase accountability.
The [values exercise](../exercises/values-clarification.md) remains available when
the user wants to clarify a direction rather than examine an emotion.

## Limits and switching

Do not automatically name “armor,” diagnose perfectionism, or label activity as
numbing. Ask whether the concept fits. Disclosure is a choice; someone need not
tell another person a painful story to prove courage. If terminology feels imposed,
return to their words or use [self-compassion](self-compassion.md).

This repository's prompts are conversational adaptations. Recognizing a phrase
does not establish why a person feels shame or whether a clinical intervention helps.
