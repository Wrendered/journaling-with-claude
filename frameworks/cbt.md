# Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT)

**Purpose:** Examine a specific interpretation or behavior that may be keeping a
problem going, then test a more workable response. This reference supports
reflection and small experiments in conversation; it is not a treatment protocol.

## Model and assumptions

CBT considers relationships among situations, interpretations, emotions, bodily
responses, and behavior. Thoughts can influence feelings; they are not the sole
cause of feelings. Physical states, loss, danger, relationships, and circumstances
also matter. The cognitive model is a working formulation to revise with evidence,
not proof that distress is a thinking error. Contemporary Beck CBT includes goals,
strengths, context, and collaborative decisions. See the
[Beck Institute overview](https://beckinstitute.org/about/understanding-cbt/).

An automatic thought is a thought that arises readily. Its speed or emotional
intensity does not establish whether it is accurate. Labels such as
all-or-nothing thinking, overgeneralization, mind-reading, and catastrophizing
describe possible patterns to examine, not verdicts to impose.

## When this lens fits

- The user wants to examine a prediction, harsh judgment, or repeated assumption.
- A manageable action could produce useful information about a stuck belief.
- Avoidance or inactivity is narrowing choices, and the user wants practical help.
- A difficult situation needs both acknowledgment and a clearer next step.

The conversational use cases here are design choices, not clinical indications.
Start with listening when the user wants to vent or be understood. When a concern
is accurate, problem-solving, boundaries, support, or grief may be more useful than
reframing. Repeatedly proving a worry false can itself become an unhelpful loop.

## What AI can borrow conversationally

Choose one method at a time and ask one question at a time. These are brief
adaptations, not a requirement to complete a worksheet.

**A short thought record:**

1. Establish the event in the user's words, including what remains unknown.
2. Separate the feeling, bodily experience, thought, and response without insisting
   that the user can identify all four.
3. Examine evidence that supports the interpretation as well as evidence that
   limits it. Do not invent reassuring explanations.
4. Invite a fairer statement that the user actually believes. It can retain
   uncertainty and acknowledge a real problem.
5. Ask whether that changes the next action or how the situation feels. No change
   is useful feedback, not failure.

**A small behavioral experiment:** State a prediction, choose a reversible action
within the user's control, decide what observable result would be informative,
and review what happened. A single result may leave several explanations open.
Do not turn another person's response into a test of the user's worth.

**Activity planning:** Help choose one feasible activity connected to enjoyment,
connection, care, or accomplishment. Consider energy, disability, and practical
constraints. Review usefulness rather than demanding productivity or promising
that motivation will follow action.

Useful prompts include: “What did you observe, and what did you infer?” and
“What would be a fair account that includes the difficult facts?”

## Pitfalls and limits

- Validate the experience before examining a conclusion. Do not label emotions
  irrational or argue away discrimination, mistreatment, or actual danger.
- Offer interpretations tentatively. Do not infer a hidden core belief or diagnose
  a disorder from recurring journal language.
- Mood ratings and tracking are optional. Stop if they intensify rumination or
  become another performance demand.
- Clinical exposure is not reduced here to “stay until anxiety falls.” This
  reference does not guide trauma processing or unsupervised exposure treatment.
- Change lenses when thought-checking becomes an argument. [ACT](act.md) may help
  when the useful move is choosing an action while uncertainty remains.

## Evidence scope

Clinical CBT and cognitive therapy have been tested in structured care. In a
240-person depression trial, cognitive therapy and medication had the same
16-week response rate, with important site and therapist-experience differences
([DeRubeis et al., 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15809408/)). Another trial
of 241 adults found that, among those with more severe depression, behavioral
activation and medication outperformed cognitive therapy
([Dimidjian et al., 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16881773/)). These selected
findings support attention to the particular intervention and setting, not a
universal ranking or a claim that changing thoughts explains all improvement.

These studies did not test this repository, ordinary journaling, or a general AI
assistant. Borrowing a technique does not transfer a clinical effect size or
establish conversational effectiveness.
