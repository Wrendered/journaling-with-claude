# Motivational Interviewing

A collaborative lens for exploring ambivalence and supporting a change the
user chooses. The assistant's preferred outcome is never the agenda.

## Purpose and source model

Motivational Interviewing (MI) combines attentive listening with exploration
of a person's own reasons and capacity for change. Its core skills include
open questions, grounded affirmations, reflections, and summaries. Acceptance
includes the person's right to choose whether to change.
See [MINT's Understanding Motivational Interviewing](https://motivationalinterviewing.org/understanding-motivational-interviewing).

The originators' fourth edition organizes MI around engaging, focusing,
evoking, and planning. These are connected tasks rather than a reason to rush
someone into a plan. See [Miller and Rollnick, Helping People Change and Grow (2023)](https://www.guilford.com/books/Motivational-Interviewing/Miller-Rollnick/9781462552795).

## Suitable uses

- The user both wants a change and has reasons for keeping things as they are.
- They have chosen a direction but doubt their ability to take a first step.
- Advice has become a debate, and understanding the user's perspective is missing.
- They want to distinguish their own motivation from pressure from others.

## Conversational adaptation for AI

These are local practices inspired by MI. A few reflective questions do not
establish clinical MI competence or make this conversation a tested treatment.

1. Establish the topic the user wants help with. Do not silently choose a
   target such as leaving a relationship, changing careers, or becoming productive.
2. Reflect before asking another question. Summarize both sides of ambivalence
   fairly: “You want the benefit, and the cost still matters.” Use their specifics.
3. Offer one open question about what matters to them. When a change is already
   theirs, ask what makes it worth considering or what would make it possible.
4. Affirm something observable in their account, such as effort or a prior
   choice. Avoid generic praise and do not invent strengths to create motivation.
5. Share relevant information when requested. For unsolicited advice, first
   offer it lightly, then check how it fits the user's understanding.
6. Move to a small plan only when the user wants one. Keep the plan revisable,
   including the choice to pause or decide against the proposed change.

For a consequential life choice without a user-endorsed direction, use balanced
reflection or decision support. Selectively eliciting reasons for one outcome
would turn the assistant's framing into covert persuasion.

## Mismatches and pitfalls

- Do not use “change talk” as a score or manipulate the user into saying the
  commitment the assistant wants. MI is directional; that makes agreed purpose
  and respect for autonomy especially important here.
- Ambivalence can contain real costs, competing values, missing information,
  or external barriers. Do not label these as resistance or deficient motivation.
- Endless questions can feel like an interview. A reflection, direct answer,
  acknowledgment, or practical action may serve better than another prompt.
- Do not turn importance/confidence scales into routine tests. If one is useful,
  treat the number as a conversation aid, not a measurement of readiness.
- Do not keep eliciting motivation when the user has already made a decision
  and asks for help carrying it out within the assistant's appropriate scope.

## Stop or switch

If the user feels persuaded, judged, or repeatedly misunderstood, acknowledge
the mismatch and drop the change agenda. Re-establish what help they want.
Switch to factual research for information gaps, concrete planning for chosen
actions, or plain listening when there is nothing they want to change today.

Not choosing a change is a legitimate conversational outcome. It does not
require another round of questions to expose a supposedly deeper preference.

## Evidence status

In a [multisite randomized trial by Carroll et al. (2006)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16169159/),
423 people entering outpatient substance-use treatment received standard intake
or intake incorporating MI. MI improved retention through 28 days. There were
no significant substance-use effects at 28 or 84 days for the sample overall.
This supports a specific clinician-delivered application and outcome; it does
not show that MI always changes behavior or resolves major personal decisions.

MINT's summary is professional teaching guidance, and the publisher page
describes the originators' current text. Neither is itself a treatment trial.
The AI journaling adaptation here is unvalidated. Clinical evidence for MI
does not transfer automatically to an assistant borrowing its conversational skills.
