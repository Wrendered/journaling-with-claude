# Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT)

**Purpose:** Help choose meaningful action when difficult thoughts or feelings
remain present. The conversational adaptation focuses on values, flexible
attention, and workable next steps, without requiring certainty or immediate
emotional relief.

## Model and assumptions

ACT, developed by Steven Hayes and colleagues, targets psychological flexibility:
being responsive to the present situation while acting in line with chosen
values. Its six processes are acceptance, cognitive defusion, present-moment
attention, self-as-context, values, and committed action. These form a theoretical
and clinical framework, not six requirements every conversation must meet. See
[Hayes's account for ACBS](https://contextualscience.org/book/export/html/240).

In plain language, defusion means noticing a thought without automatically
obeying it; self-as-context involves perspective-taking on experience. Neither
requires claiming that thoughts are false or that the person has a metaphysical
“true self.” Acceptance concerns willingness to experience what is present; it
does not imply approving an external situation.

## When this lens fits

- The user is waiting to feel confident, certain, or motivated before acting.
- Arguing with a recurring thought is consuming attention without helping.
- The user wants to distinguish an outcome goal from how they want to live.
- A decision involves unavoidable discomfort and competing priorities.

These uses are designed conversational support. Do not assume that reluctance is
experiential avoidance: it can reflect exhaustion, competing needs, a bad plan,
or real risk. Sometimes the next useful move is rest, factual investigation,
practical assistance, or a different goal.

## What AI can borrow conversationally

Choose the smallest useful move and keep the user's wording and choices central.
The following prompts are original adaptations rather than a clinical sequence.

**Values clarification:** Ask what matters about the choice and what quality the
user wants to bring to it. Separate a chosen direction from a finish line or
someone else's expectation. Values may conflict; do not invent a hierarchy.

**Light defusion:** Invite the user to phrase a recurring prediction as “I'm
having the thought that…” if that gives them room to choose. Do not repeat it as
a compulsory exercise, mock the thought, or imply that factual checking no longer
matters. A thought can contain useful information and still be an unhelpful rule.

**Workability check:** Explore what a current strategy offers immediately and
what it costs over time. Include material realities and relationships, not only
internal feelings. Treat the answer as a question to investigate together.

**Present attention:** Briefly notice the current task or surroundings if that
helps. Inward or breath-focused attention is optional; it should not become a
test of willingness or a demand to endure distress.

**Committed action:** Help choose one feasible step that expresses a stated value.
Identify what support it needs and when to review it. Changing or stopping a plan
can be flexible action, too. Evaluate what was learned, not whether the user
eliminated discomfort or met an externally imposed standard.

An optional experiment: choose a value, name one thought that might show up, and
try a small action that does not require that thought to disappear. Afterwards,
ask whether the action served the value and what the user would adjust.

## Pitfalls and limits

- Acceptance must not become pressure to tolerate abuse, unsafe conditions, or
  preventable harm. External change and protection remain valid options.
- Do not use “values” to push productivity, a relationship outcome, or the
  assistant's preferred life decision. Rest and connection can matter as much as
  achievement; the user supplies the priorities.
- Do not blame persistent distress on insufficient willingness. Relief is a valid
  desire, and changing feelings or seeking support can also be useful.
- Do not assert that all suffering comes from avoidance or that flexibility is the
  uniquely proven cause of improvement. Model mechanisms and clinical outcomes
  are different kinds of claims.
- This adaptation does not guide exposure or trauma processing. When a metaphor
  feels dismissive or abstract, return to ordinary language and concrete needs.
- [CBT](cbt.md) can help examine a claim's accuracy. ACT can help choose what to
  do when further argument about that claim has stopped being useful.

## Evidence scope

A randomized trial of 128 people beginning treatment for mixed anxiety disorders
found similar overall improvement with ACT and CBT; both included exposure.
Follow-up differences varied by outcome and analysis, with additional therapy use
also differing between groups ([Arch et al., 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22563639/)).
This supports ACT as a clinical option in that setting, not universal superiority,
proof of every proposed mechanism, or a treatment-selection rule for an individual.

That study tested structured psychotherapy. It did not evaluate this repository,
performance coaching, ordinary journaling, or a general AI assistant. Its results
do not establish that isolated values or defusion prompts have the same benefits.
